# Reference: signs of AI writing

Distilled from Wikipedia's [Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)
(the parts applicable to general prose — its wikitext/citation/edit-summary
sections are Wikipedia-specific and omitted here) and 2026 sources on AI-slop
tells. Use this as a checklist to apply with judgment, not a mechanical
find-and-replace list — several of these words and constructions are
perfectly fine in genuinely on-topic use. The question is always whether the
word or pattern is doing real work in this sentence, or just filling space.

## Vocabulary to view with suspicion

Words that show up constantly in AI output regardless of topic — a signal to
check whether a plainer, more specific word would say the same thing:

delve, leverage, navigate, elevate, intricate, meticulously, synergy, empower,
landscape, ecosystem, underscore, seamless, robust, game-changer, crucial,
pivotal, testament, tapestry, showcasing, boasts, vibrant, garner, foster/
fostering, additionally, bolstered, enduring, interplay, key, valuable,
groundbreaking, renowned, diverse array, profound, exemplifies, commitment to,
nestled, dive into, here's the kicker, making waves, in the heart of.

**Before:** "This meticulously crafted approach showcases a commitment to
robust, seamless integration."
**After:** "This integrates cleanly and took real care to get right."

## Copula-avoidance

Replacing plain "is/are" with a marketing-flavored verb: "serves as," "stands
as," "marks," "functions as," "operates as," "represents," "features,"
"offers," "maintains" — where a neutral "is" or "has" would be more direct.

**Before:** "The dashboard serves as a central hub that offers real-time
visibility."
**After:** "The dashboard shows real-time status in one place."

## Vague connection language

"In connection with," "connected with/to," "in association with," "associated
with" used instead of a precise preposition or a direct statement of the
relationship.

**Before:** "Issues associated with the deployment process."
**After:** "Deployment issues." / "Issues in the deployment process."

## Negative parallelism — the single most common tell

"It's not just X, it's Y." "It's not X, it's Y." "X rather than Y" used as a
rhetorical flourish rather than because a real contrast is being drawn. Cited
across nearly every source as the most instantly recognizable AI pattern.

**Before:** "It's not just a tool, it's a productivity system."
**After:** "It also handles scheduling and reporting, not just tracking."

## Rule-of-three overuse

Reflexively grouping things in threes for rhythm, even when two or four would
be the honest count.

**Before:** "The update improves speed, reliability, and usability."
(fine if genuinely three things — the tell is doing this in nearly every
sentence, padding to hit three when the real list is shorter or longer.)

## Undue-significance filler

Generic claims about importance or legacy instead of a specific fact: "is a
testament to," "underscores the importance of," "reflects broader," "key
turning point," "evolving landscape."

**Before:** "This decision is a testament to the team's commitment to
quality."
**After:** "The team rewrote the module twice before shipping it."

## Present-participle tacking

A trailing "-ing" clause bolted onto a sentence to imply significance without
adding a fact: "...,highlighting the importance of...", "...,underscoring...",
"...,fostering...", "...,ensuring...", "...,reflecting...".

**Before:** "The team shipped the fix early, underscoring their dedication to
quality."
**After:** "The team shipped the fix a week early."

## Promotional / press-release tone

"Boasts a," "vibrant," "rich," "showcasing," "exemplifies," "groundbreaking,"
"renowned," "diverse array of" — travel-guide or marketing-copy register
where a neutral, factual one is expected.

**Before:** "Nestled in a vibrant, diverse array of neighborhoods, the city
boasts a rich cultural scene."
**After:** "The city has distinct neighborhoods, each with its own food and
music scene."

## The "challenges and future prospects" formula

A rigid structure: "Despite its [strengths], X faces challenges... Looking
ahead, X is expected to..." Recognizable as a template regardless of subject
matter. If a piece needs to cover limitations and next steps, do it with
specifics, not this shape.

## Fake rhetorical questions

Questions asked purely for structure, not because they invite real reflection:
"What does this mean? Faster results." Just state the point.

## Connector-word chains

Nearly every sentence opening with "Additionally," "Furthermore," "Moreover" —
mechanical stacking instead of the sentences' content doing the connective
work.

## Hyperbole and drama

"This changes everything." "We're witnessing history." Claims sized to
manufacture excitement rather than match the actual stakes.

## Faux-candor tags

"And honestly?" and similar tags used to simulate warmth or vulnerability
rather than to say anything new.

## Filler / "content-shaped air"

Paragraphs that are structurally complete — topic sentence, elaboration,
example — but convey nothing a reader didn't already know: "In today's
fast-paced digital landscape, creators need powerful solutions..." After
writing or revising a paragraph, ask: what did the reader just learn? If
nothing, cut it.

## Default recap paragraphs

A closing paragraph that just restates what the piece already said. Cut it
unless the piece is long enough that a reader genuinely needs the reminder.

## Symmetrical, uniform structure

Every section the same length, every paragraph following the same
topic/explain/example/wrap-up shape, sentences clustering in the same length
range. The absence of natural rhythm variance is itself a tell — real writing
is uneven.

## Uniform paragraph-opener formula

Varied sentence-length rhythm *within* paragraphs can still sit on top of a
rigid pattern *between* them: every paragraph opening with a short, flat
declarative "mini-thesis" sentence before elaborating. E.g. a five-paragraph
piece opening each paragraph with "That phrase is...", "The word itself is
just...", "That happened around...", "The word outlived...", "What keeps X in
circulation is...". Each sentence is fine alone; the repeated shape across
every paragraph is the tell. Vary how paragraphs open — some with a fact,
some with a name, some with a fragment, some by continuing straight from the
prior paragraph with no fresh topic sentence at all.

## Circular / epigrammatic closer

Ending a piece by circling back to the opening tension or image with a tidy,
slightly ironic callback: "X wasn't trying to do Y, but it turned out to be
great at exactly that." This full-circle button is an extremely common AI
essay signature — it makes the piece feel "finished" a little too neatly.
Genuine human writing more often ends on a substantive point, a specific
detail, a shrug, or just stops without the bow on top. If a draft's last
sentence explicitly rhymes with its first, that's a signal to cut it or
replace it with something plainer.

## Formatting tells

- Em dashes. Avoid by default, not just "in moderation." Outside
  professionally copyedited prose, most people essentially never type one —
  it isn't on a standard keyboard, so a human writing casually reaches for a
  comma, colon, parenthesis, or a period and a new sentence instead. Even two
  or three in a short piece read as a tell; treat any em dash as worth
  rewriting away rather than budgeting a "reasonable" amount.
- Title-case headings ("Like This") where sentence case is the norm.
- Excessive bold, used to simulate emphasis rather than mark something that
  actually needs to stand out.
- Emoji used as section dividers.
- Nested headings with no body text underneath, or a heading immediately
  followed by a bullet list with no transitional sentence.
- Smart/curly quotes and apostrophes where plain typed punctuation is
  expected in the context.
- Horizontal-rule dividers between every section.
- Numbers spelled out oddly ("seven" instead of "7"), or suspiciously round
  hedged counts ("a diverse array of tools") avoiding a real number.

## Signs of human writing, for positive contrast

- Specific, contextual language tied to the actual subject rather than a
  generic template.
- Atypical syntax — sentences that don't all resolve the same way.
- Coherent, traceable reasoning instead of generic framing.
- Uneven rhythm: some short sentences, some long, without a pattern to it.
- A clear position stated directly, rather than hedged both-sides framing.
